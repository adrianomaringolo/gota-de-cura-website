Anyone cutting a blood orange for the first time tends to step back. On the outside it looks like an ordinary orange, sometimes with a reddish blush on the peel. Inside, the flesh runs from pink to wine, as if someone had painted the fruit. The smell, though, is pure orange: sweet, fresh, with a slightly fruity undertone. This text tells where it comes from, why it has that color, what is different about our distillation and how to use the hydrosol safely.

## What the blood orange is

The blood orange is the same species as the sweet orange we all know: *Citrus sinensis*, in the **rue family (Rutaceae)**, home to lemons, mandarins, grapefruit and bergamot. It is not a separate species, but a group of sweet orange varieties that accumulate a red pigment in the flesh and, sometimes, in the peel.

The sweet orange itself is ancient, a natural cross between the mandarin and the pomelo that happened in **southern China and neighboring regions**. From there it followed trade routes to the Mediterranean, and Portuguese sailors played a big part in spreading it around the world, Brazil included.

## Where the color comes from

The red comes from **anthocyanins**, water-soluble pigments that color blueberries, blackberries, red grapes and red cabbage. Common oranges do not make them. Blood oranges do, because of a **natural genetic mutation**: researchers at the John Innes Centre in the UK showed in 2012 that a mobile piece of DNA (a retrotransposon) settled next to a gene called *Ruby*, which controls the production of these pigments.

The curious part is that this switch **only turns on with cold**. Without cool nights during ripening, the gene stays quiet and the flesh comes out orange, almost like a regular orange. That is why the blood orange is tied to so few places in the world.

## Origin: Sicily and Mount Etna

The blood orange most people talk about was born in **Sicily**, Italy, and the area around **Mount Etna** is still the main producing region: warm days and cold nights on volcanic soil are exactly what the *Ruby* gene asks for. Italians sometimes call it the island's "red gold".

When the mutation first appeared is an open question. Some link the fruit to the Arab period in Sicily, from the 9th century on, but that date comes more from popular writing than primary sources, and the safest records are much later. What the genetic studies point to is that the color arose **more than once**, in different places: the Sicilian origin, another linked to Spain, and a third in China.

The three best-known varieties are:

- **Tarocco.** The Sicilian favorite, sweet, low in acidity, with flesh streaked in red.
- **Moro.** The darkest, with deep wine-colored flesh and a bolder flavor that hints at red berries.
- **Sanguinello.** Of Spanish origin, with a redder peel and flesh marked with color.

Outside Sicily it grows in places like Spain, the United States, Japan, South Africa and Iran, but the color does not show up every year. In climates that are too warm, the fruit can ripen without pigmenting.

## The plant and the fruit

The orange tree is small to medium in size, with glossy dark green leaves and very fragrant white flowers, the orange blossoms that give rise to another well-known scent. The fruit is rich in **vitamin C** and, in the blood orange, also carries anthocyanins, antioxidants studied in the lab. A word of honesty, as always: eating the fruit is one thing, using the hydrosol is another. The benefits of the flesh do not automatically carry over into the bottle.

## We distill the leaves, not the peel

At Gota de Cura, blood orange hydrosol and essential oil come from distilling the **leaves** of the orange tree, not the fruit or its peel. That matters for anyone using it on the skin.

Photosensitivity is an exaggerated skin reaction to sunlight after contact with certain substances, the furanocoumarins. They turn up in some citrus oils taken from the peel, especially the cold-pressed ones, which is why people are told to avoid the sun after using them. Because our material comes from the leaves, **neither the hydrosol nor the essential oil is photosensitizing**, which keeps skin use safe. For a deeper look, see Via Aroma's article on [photosensitizing or phototoxic essential oils](https://viaaroma.blog/2022/04/05/o-que-sao-oleos-essenciais-fotossensibilizantes-ou-fototoxicos/) (in Portuguese).

## Blood orange hydrosol

Distillation gives two products at once. The **essential oil** carries the fat-soluble substances, and the **hydrosol** carries the water-soluble ones, in a lighter, gentler profile, with the plant's fresh citrus character and little of the oil's concentration. We explain the difference in more detail in [essential oil or hydrosol, what is the difference](/en/blog/oleos-essenciais-e-hidrolatos-diferencas).

What is attributed to blood orange hydrosol comes mostly from the **traditional use of citrus** and from the experience of people who use it. There are no clinical studies specifically on the hydrosol of this variety.

### For body and mind

- **Invigorating.** The citrus scent is associated with a feeling of energy and alertness. A mist on the face or in the air, in the morning or on a tired afternoon, tends to be welcome.
- **Calming.** At the same time, the sweet orange scent is traditionally tied to relaxation. In aromatherapy, citrus shows up both to lift and to calm, and the response varies from person to person. For why a smell moves emotion, see [aromatherapy and the limbic system](/en/blog/aromaterapia-e-sistema-limbico).

### For the skin

- **Facial toner.** Gentle enough to mist on the face after cleansing, to refresh and prepare the skin for moisturizer. Suitable for most skin types, but test a small area first.
- **Cleansing and freshness.** By traditional use it is considered mildly antiseptic, handy for refreshing the skin. That does not make it a treatment for infection or acne.
- **Light deodorant.** Misted on the underarms after a shower, it helps keep the skin fresh and scented. It is not an antiperspirant: it perfumes and refreshes, it does not stop sweat.

### For digestion

Some people take the hydrosol diluted in water after meals, following the traditional use of citrus. Caution applies here: **internal use of hydrosol only with water of known origin and professional guidance**. It is not something we recommend on your own.

### For your space

Misted into the air, on bed linen or in the car, or in an ultrasonic diffuser, it is a simple way to bring the scent of orange into everyday life.

## The vibrational view

Beyond use on the body, aromatherapy has a symbolic reading of plants, the so-called vibrational view. It belongs to tradition and sensitivity, not to scientific proof, and that is how we present it. Marcelo, the aromatherapist in charge of Gota de Cura, sums up what this reading says about the blood orange:

> "In the vibrational view of aromatherapy, we associate the blood orange with stimulation, forgiveness, letting go of hurt and resentment, the renewal of feelings, making room for acceptance and a lighter heart."

For him, the hydrosol is above all an invitation to turn self-care into a moment of kindness toward yourself: to breathe, notice how you feel and allow space for brighter moments and lighter feelings. One mist on the face, eyes closed, can already be that moment.

## The essential oil: already diluted and ready to use

We also offer blood orange essential oil (*Citrus sinensis*) in a **ready-diluted** version: **blood orange essential oil (2% dilution in MCT), 5 ml bottle**. That is two drops of essential oil for every 5 ml of MCT, medium-chain triglyceride, a light fractionated vegetable oil with almost no scent. You do not have to do the math or count drops: the dilution is already done. To understand how and why we dilute, see our page on [essential oil dilutions](/en/diluicoes-oleos-essenciais).

A 2% dilution is commonly suggested for use on the body, in massage and for localized application. Even so, do a patch test first, keep it away from eyes, ears and mucous membranes, and remember that, because it is made from leaves and not peel, our oil is not photosensitizing. Essential oil must **never be ingested**.

## What the chromatography shows

We sent the essential oil for gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-MS), the technique that separates and identifies each substance present. The report, from October 2024, is a qualitative analysis, and the percentages are each compound's share of the total chromatogram area. About twenty compounds were identified, and three of them make up more than 60% of the total:

- **Citronellal**, about 24%, with a fresh citrus scent.
- **Linalool**, about 20%, floral and soft.
- **D-limonene**, about 20%, the best-known orange scent.

In smaller amounts there are sabinene (about 7.6%), benzyl benzoate, trans-beta-ocimene, geranial and neral (about 3 to 5% each). It is quite different from orange peel oil, where limonene is usually almost the whole story: the leaf oil has more linalool and citronellal, which fits the greener, more floral scent of our bottle. See the full report on our [chromatography page](/en/cromatografias).

## Safety precautions

- **It is water, and it spoils.** Keep it in the fridge, in a bottle with a spray valve (so you do not wet your finger), away from light, and discard if it turns cloudy or the smell changes.
- **Do a patch test** on a small area of skin before first use and wait a few hours, especially if you are allergic to citrus.
- **Avoid eyes, ears and mucous membranes.**
- **Essential oil needs more care** than the hydrosol: always diluted and never ingested. Ours is not photosensitizing because it comes from leaves. Peel-based citrus oils from other sources can stain skin exposed to the sun, so check the origin if you use another one.
- **Pets.** Citrus scents can bother cats and dogs. Be careful with diffusers and sprays in closed rooms where they stay.

This text does not replace an individual assessment. **If in doubt, always consult a professional**, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, thinking of using it with small children or have a diagnosed health condition.
